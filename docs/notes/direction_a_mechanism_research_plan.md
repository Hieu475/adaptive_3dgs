# Direction A: Compute dilution as a measurable failure mechanism

## Recommendation

Make Direction A the primary research question. Treat joint spawn–refine control as a possible consequence of the mechanism study, not as the paper's starting claim.

**Proposed paper question:** Under a fixed online mapping resource budget, when does map growth dilute optimization enough to leave useful Gaussians under-refined, and does a targeted reallocation of the same measured compute prevent the resulting quality loss?

This is a mechanism paper with an intervention. It is not a claim that a particular mature quota is universally optimal.

The strongest result today is therefore **a causal local effect of update allocation**, not a complete causal chain from Gaussian growth through starvation to long-horizon quality loss. The main open link is mediation: show that growth under a fixed resource cap causes a measurable reduction in useful update exposure, and that restoring this exposure rescues later quality without changing unrelated factors.

## Hypotheses to test

- **H1 — Dilution:** As the active eligible map grows under a fixed measured mapping budget, the rate at which useful, visible Gaussians receive updates falls.
- **H2 — Prediction:** Mature-update coverage and per-Gaussian update interval predict quality loss over a prespecified future horizon, after accounting for current residual, visibility, new-surface ratio, map size, and motion.
- **H3 — Intervention:** On a common map snapshot, allocating more of the same work to under-served useful Gaussians improves later reference-view quality when the measured starvation signal is high. It need not help every scene or every frame.
- **H4 — Resource constraint:** The intervention improves quality at equal measured mapping time, or improves quality per millisecond under a latency cap. Equal micro-update count is a separate, weaker systems estimand and must be reported separately.

Use **useful eligible Gaussian** explicitly. Age ≥10 frames can remain a baseline definition, but it is only a proxy: age does not imply visibility, importance, or unresolved error. Record at least (a) visible mature candidates, (b) selected/updated visible mature candidates, (c) each candidate's frames since last update, and (d) its pre-update residual/attribution. Report both population coverage and visible-candidate coverage so the denominator is interpretable.

## Decisive experiment sequence

### A1. Validate the exposure measure

Replay fixed map snapshots and fixed candidate pools with several controlled budgets. Change only the update budget and age/visibility mix. Confirm that the proposed coverage and update-interval metrics change monotonically with the assigned budget, and that independent reruns preserve the direction. This catches scheduler saturation and empty-candidate failures before a quality claim is made.

### A2. Same-snapshot causal dose response

At preregistered early, transition, and late snapshots, branch from the exact same map, optimizer state, pose, reference render, and candidate pool. Compare global-importance allocation with a small prespecified dose curve (for example 25%, 50%, 75% mature work), while holding the total work fixed. Add a matched-wall-time block with CUDA synchronization; do not treat it as interchangeable with the fixed-update block.

Primary outcome: quality change on a fixed set of current and short-horizon replay views. Secondary outcomes: depth and geometry, mature coverage, update intervals, map count, synchronized optimization time, and full-frame latency. Reject cells where the requested allocation or candidate pool cannot be realized; report the failure rate as a result.

### A3. Long-horizon rescue

Use the A1/A2 evidence to select one simple, frozen starvation intervention. Run it continuously against global importance and the current throttling policy on the same trajectory/pose/initialization, with seed as the independent cluster. Include both a fixed-update comparison and a measured-latency comparison. Test first on scenes already used for development as replication, then on genuinely untouched sequences for generalization. The repository's existing scene-exposure audit says the local sequences are already exposed; they cannot be relabeled as new held-outs.

### A4. Predictive test without controller tuning

Freeze a simple predictor on development scenes: for example, whether low useful-update coverage or a long update interval predicts the next K-frame quality drop beyond current residual and scene novelty. Evaluate once on untouched sequences. Compare against a constant base-rate predictor and residual-only predictor. This establishes whether the mechanism is forecastable without prematurely introducing an MLP/router.

## Preregistered gates

1. **Measurement gate:** eligibility and coverage denominators are logged consistently; requested and realized budgets agree; snapshot, candidate, pose, and reference-render hashes match.
2. **Causal gate:** the same-snapshot intervention changes useful update exposure and the subsequent quality endpoint in the predicted direction, with seed-cluster uncertainty. Report null and opposite-sign scenes.
3. **Mediation gate:** under controlled growth/budget conditions, growth pressure changes exposure; exposure change precedes the quality change; the rescue intervention attenuates the quality loss. If this chain fails, narrow the claim to “age-aware allocation affects local mapping quality.”
4. **Systems gate:** report CUDA-event stage time and synchronized full-frame p50/p95/p99 latency. Claim a resource-constrained benefit only if quality improves under the stated measured budget.
5. **Generalization gate:** at least one development/test split by sequence, frozen before evaluation. Existing exposed TUM/Replica sequences are not a valid untouched test set.

## Paper claim boundaries

**Claim if only A2 succeeds:** “Age/visibility-aware update allocation has a causal local effect on online Gaussian map quality under controlled compute.”

**Claim only if A1–A4 succeed:** “Compute dilution is a measurable and predictive failure mechanism, and a simple intervention mitigates its long-horizon quality cost under a measured resource constraint.”

Do not claim a universal mature quota, a joint spawn–refine optimum, real-time operation from modeled budget, or estimated-pose SLAM performance without the corresponding experiments.

## Product path

The first product-shaped component can stay simple: a mapper-side monitor that reports update coverage, age since last useful update, growth pressure, latency, and quality-return diagnostics, plus a configurable starvation guard. It should expose why it allocated work and degrade safely when a requested age group has too few eligible candidates. A joint spawn/refine controller is a later option if A2 demonstrates a stable action-value difference. This creates a useful systems component even if the paper finds that starvation matters only in specific regimes.

The research area is active: [SPARE-GS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.16624) studies global budget-constrained Gaussian allocation in 3DGS, and [GaussLite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30809) studies task-conditioned resource allocation for online robotic mapping. The paper should position its contribution around causal measurement, prediction, and intervention under online mapping constraints, rather than claiming priority for adaptive allocation alone.

## Recommended next concrete task

Before another controller or broad benchmark, implement the A1/A2 logging and preregistration changes on a **new, unexposed sequence**: useful-visible candidate denominators, last-update age, per-age residual, exact realized quota, snapshot/candidate hashes, and synchronized stage time. Then run a small feasibility pilot only; freeze the complete seed/scene protocol before the inferential batch. If no new sequence can be sourced, run this as exploratory mechanism replication and make no held-out generalization claim.

## Expanded trajectory replication (exploratory, exposed scenes)

Growth treatment was the existing coverage throttle on/off, with the same error-only selector. Allocation was global versus a 50% stale-mature rescue (age ≥10 frames and last update ≥10 frames). All 32 arms per scene passed the runner's protocol checks. Full logs, manifests, fixed-view evaluations, seed-bootstrap intervals, and timing measurements are in [Replica office1 report](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/expanded_replication/replica_office1/report.md) and [TUM fr1 rpy report](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/expanded_replication/tum_fr1_rpy/report.md). These are exploratory replications on exposed scenes, not held-out results.

In both scenes, the intervention sharply reduced measured stale-mature starvation while using the same requested update count: on office1, starvation prevalence was about 0.79→0.22 for unthrottled growth and 0.66→0.14 for throttled growth; on TUM rpy it was about 0.87→0.18 and 0.75→0.16, respectively. Growth throttling also reduced births by roughly half. These treatment effects establish that the interventions changed the intended exposure and growth quantities, but the quality outcomes are metric- and scene-dependent. In particular, fixed-view rescue gains alongside training-view losses indicate that the endpoints measure different behavior and must both remain visible in any claim.

The factorial does **not** yet establish mediation from growth through starvation to quality: growth and allocation changed throughout different trajectories, rescue did not consistently attenuate the growth-associated quality difference, and no matched snapshot replay or held-out sequence is included here. The evidence supports continuing Direction A as a conditional mechanism study, while narrowing the current positive claim to: growth and update allocation measurably alter mature update exposure, with scene-dependent quality consequences under a fixed micro-update count. The next decisive experiment remains same-snapshot or matched-state growth intervention followed by a fixed-view short-horizon endpoint, then a genuinely untouched sequence if available.

## Same-snapshot horizon intervention

To allow spawned Gaussians to age into the mature/stale population, the same-snapshot runner was extended: branch from a shared frame-30 or frame-90 map, add 0 or 3,584 Gaussians once, process 15 later frames at 5,000 updates/frame with no further births, then re-render fixed pre-intervention views. All arms begin from an identical map and use the same data/pose prefix. The experiment is still exploratory and on the exposed `tum_fr1_rpy` sequence. See the [frame-30 four-seed feasibility pilot](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/matched_state_early_snapshot_pilot/report.md), its [eight-seed seed replication](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/matched_state_early_snapshot_replication/report.md), and the [frame-90 two-seed pilot](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/matched_state_horizon_pilot/report.md).

At frame 30, the eight-seed replication reproduced the exposure direction: 3,584 births reduced global mature-update coverage by 0.119 [seed-bootstrap 95% CI: −0.121, −0.117] and increased the stale/mature eligible ratio by 0.074 [0.070, 0.078]. The fixed-view quality effect of growth itself was small (+0.045 dB [0.015, 0.084]). The stale-rescue request reduced stale/mature ratio by 0.234 [−0.239, −0.229] at zero growth and 0.291 [−0.295, −0.287] at high growth. It improved fixed-view PSNR in both conditions (+0.712 dB [0.678, 0.748] and +0.589 dB [0.550, 0.623]), while the growth-by-rescue interaction was −0.124 dB [−0.167, −0.078]. Thus rescue helps retention at this snapshot, but the benefit is not stronger under growth; the factorial does not support the claim that rescue mediates a growth-caused quality loss. The growth intervention can also add useful representation support.

The frame-90 pilot had a different response: global mature coverage fell 0.040, stale/mature ratio barely changed (−0.008), and the high-dose arm gained +7.558 dB relative to zero-dose change on fixed prefix views. This large apparent benefit is consistent with direct representation support and cannot be read as evidence for or against a subtle starvation cost. Together, the frame-30 and frame-90 results support strong stage dependence.

The requested stale quota was 50% (2,500 of 5,000 micro-updates), but the scheduler can assign only up to `max_k` updates per eligible stale candidate and transfers the remainder to other candidates. It reached the exact stale quota on only 20% of frames in low-growth arms and 32% in high-growth arms at frame 30; realized stale work was 23% and 30% of total work, respectively. At frame 90, exact quota compliance was 40% of frames in both growth conditions. Every arm still used exactly 5,000 total micro-updates/frame. Future reports must distinguish the requested quota from the realized stale update share.

Synchronized optimization time was about 89–92 ms/frame and full frame time about 99–103 ms in the frame-30 replication, despite the 15 ms modeled optimization budget. This is not a real-time result. The current mechanism evidence is therefore: **growth can reduce mature update coverage and raise stale exposure in one regime, and stale-directed allocation can reduce that exposure; the quality cost and its rescue remain stage-dependent and are not yet mediated consistently.** Next, the useful work is to repeat this frozen same-snapshot protocol on another scene or snapshot regime with enough stale candidates for the requested allocation, and retain the scene-specific contrasts rather than fit a controller.

### Second-scene replication: Replica office1, frame 30

The same frozen matched-state design was replicated over eight new seeds on the exposed `replica_office1` sequence; see the [per-scene report](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/matched_state_replica_office1_replication/report.md). The exposure direction replicated: the 3,584-birth intervention reduced mature-update coverage by 0.076 [seed-bootstrap 95% CI: −0.077, −0.075] and increased the stale/mature eligible ratio by 0.040 [0.039, 0.041]. The global-allocation fixed-view PSNR effect was −0.044 dB [−0.095, +0.012], which includes zero. Rescue reduced stale/mature ratio by about 0.44–0.47 and raised fixed-view PSNR by +0.429 dB at zero growth and +0.310 dB at high growth. The growth-by-rescue interaction was −0.119 dB [−0.195, −0.031], so rescue was not more beneficial under growth.

Across the frame-30 TUM and Replica runs, map growth reproducibly reduced mature coverage and raised stale exposure, while the associated fixed-view quality effect differed: small positive on TUM (+0.045 dB; +0.015 to +0.084) and near-zero/slightly negative on Replica (−0.044 dB; −0.095 to +0.012). Rescue improved fixed-view quality in both conditions, but its effect was smaller under high growth on both scenes (interaction −0.124 dB on TUM and −0.119 dB on Replica). This is not the predicted rescue pattern for a growth-induced quality loss. It suggests that coverage/starvation is a manipulable systems state, but the short-horizon quality effect is not explained by that state alone.

The quota caveat also replicated differently by scene. The 50% stale quota was exactly achieved on only 20–32% of TUM frames, with 23–30% of total updates actually going to stale Gaussians; on Replica, exact quota compliance was 52–53% of frames and realized share was 39%. Both runs still delivered exactly 5,000 total micro-updates per frame. This capacity limit is part of the intervention and must be reported; “50% stale allocation” describes the request, not the realized work. Synchronized optimization took about 89–92 ms/frame on TUM and 62 ms/frame on Replica, with full frame times about 99–103 ms and 73–75 ms, respectively—well above the modeled 15 ms budget.

**Updated claim boundary:** these matched-state experiments provide replicated causal evidence that adding Gaussians under fixed update count can lower mature update coverage and increase stale exposure at the tested stage. They do not show that this exposure change causes a quality loss or that stale rescue specifically mitigates such a loss. Direction A remains worth pursuing as a mechanism characterization paper, but its central claim should currently stop at measurable compute dilution and conditional exposure control. The quality mediation claim needs a longer horizon, additional scene/stage replication, and a quality endpoint that distinguishes representation-support gains from update-starvation costs.

### Thirty-frame endpoint extension

The same frame-30 snapshots were continued through frame 60 for four paired seeds per scene; the comparison to the 15-frame endpoint is reported in the [TUM horizon-30 report](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/tum_fr1_rpy_horizon30/report.md) and [Replica horizon-30 report](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/replica_office1_horizon30/report.md).

- **TUM fr1 rpy:** under global allocation, growth reduced mature coverage by 0.131 [−0.132, −0.131] and raised stale/mature exposure by 0.108 [0.107, 0.109]. High growth changed fixed-view PSNR by +0.138 dB [0.115, 0.166]. Rescue improved fixed-view PSNR by +0.543 dB at zero growth and +0.547 dB at high growth; interaction +0.004 dB [−0.031, +0.039].
- **Replica office1:** growth reduced mature coverage by 0.064 [−0.065, −0.063] and raised stale/mature exposure by 0.048 [0.048, 0.049]. High growth changed fixed-view PSNR by −0.092 dB [−0.134, −0.039]. Rescue improved fixed-view PSNR by +0.515 dB at zero growth and +0.452 dB at high growth; interaction −0.063 dB [−0.090, −0.036].

The exposure effects persist at 30 frames in both scenes. The quality effect remains scene-dependent, and stale rescue is not more beneficial under high growth: its interaction is near zero on TUM and negative on Replica. Extending from 15 to 30 frames causes additional fixed-prefix-view degradation in all cells, while the amount varies by treatment. These frame-30 snapshots are still short relative to mapping trajectories and remain exposed; they do not establish long-horizon generalization or a universal quality benefit.

The strongest supported statement is now: **under a fixed micro-update count, adding a substantial Gaussian cohort causally lowers the fraction of visible mature Gaussians updated and raises stale/mature exposure across two tested scenes; a stale-directed allocator reduces stale exposure, but does not consistently recover a growth-specific quality loss.** Keep Direction A focused on measurement and conditional mechanism characterization. Do not train a controller or claim quality mediation until the same result is replicated at a later snapshot/longer sequence or on a genuinely untouched scene, with enough eligible stale candidates and measured-time controls.

### Post hoc low-motion replication: TUM fr3 sitting static

The local scene audit found no genuinely unexposed sequence: available TUM and Replica scenes already appear in mapping/tracking or policy studies. Rather than label one held-out, an eight-seed matched-state horizon experiment was run on `tum_fr3_sitting_static`, which had not been used in this specific growth × stale-rescue intervention but had prior mapping/tracking exposure. The complete [post hoc report](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/matched_state_tum_sitting_static_exploratory/report.md) is explicitly exploratory.

On this low-motion prefix, 3,584 births reduced mature update coverage by 0.075 [seed-bootstrap 95% CI: −0.0752, −0.0746] and raised stale/mature exposure by 0.056 [0.0550, 0.0561]. Growth's global-allocation fixed-view PSNR effect was near zero (−0.020 dB [−0.048, +0.009]). Stale rescue reduced stale/mature exposure by 0.516 at zero growth and 0.552 at high growth. Its fixed-view PSNR effect was +0.041 dB at zero growth and +0.128 dB at high growth; the growth × rescue interaction was +0.087 dB [+0.043, +0.134], opposite to the negative interactions on dynamic TUM fr1 rpy and Replica office1 at their 15/30-frame endpoints.

The first 60 GT pose transitions measured 1.44 mm mean translation and 0.37° mean rotation per frame for sitting-static, versus 3.19 mm/2.14° for TUM fr1 rpy and 9.30 cm/7.33° for Replica office1. Pose summaries are saved as `pose_motion.json` beside each scene's report. This adds a useful candidate condition: stale rescue may have a different quality interaction in a low-motion regime, even when the main effect of growth is near zero. It remains a post hoc single-scene observation, not a validated routing rule. The requested 50% quota was exactly met on 51–58% of frames and realized stale work was 35–37% of updates, so capacity limits still matter.

**Current direction decision:** continue Direction A as a conditional mechanism study. The growth → lower mature coverage → higher stale exposure link now repeats across three scene regimes; the exposure → quality link varies by motion/scene and horizon. The strongest next step is a preregistered test on data not previously inspected for these outcomes. Since the local audit finds none, obtaining a new sequence is required before making a generalization claim; the existing local scenes can support mechanism characterization and a post hoc motion-condition hypothesis only.

The control held optimization-stage p50 near its target: 84 ms for TUM and 56–59 ms across Replica arms. Adaptive arms used different numbers of updates (roughly 5.6–6.6k on TUM and 7.3–7.6k on Replica), which is the intended compensation for stage-time cost differences. The quota request was 50%, while realized stale work was only about 32–36% on TUM and 27–28% on Replica. Do not describe the intervention as actually assigning half the measured compute to stale Gaussians. Fixed-view retention also is not novel-view generalization. The next useful work is an age/visibility dose-response from identical snapshots (including achieved vs requested quota), and a genuinely new sequence for any generalization claim; do not build a controller from these results.

At high growth, the contrasts against the corresponding global arm were less certain on TUM (+0.591 dB at requested 75%, CI −0.117 to +1.226), but stayed positive on Replica (+0.711, +0.738, +0.554 dB at 25%, 50%, 75%). Thus the dose curve does not show that higher stale quota mediates or removes the growth penalty; at the tested snapshot, more stale-directed allocation coincided with a larger growth-associated quality loss.

Two cautions matter for interpretation. First, the requested quota is not the realized update share: candidate capacity caps the realized stale share at about 52% on TUM and 36% on Replica even for a 75% request. Second, the overall mature-update coverage metric fell on TUM as the stale quota rose (0.350 global to 0.270 at 75%) even while fixed-view PSNR retention improved. This shows that broad unique mature coverage and focused stale work are not interchangeable; report age-group exposure and quality together rather than presenting one as a proxy for the other. Actual optimization-stage p50 remained around 85–87 ms on TUM and 58–60 ms on Replica; full-frame latency averaged roughly 96–97 ms and 69–70 ms, respectively.

**Direction A update:** the mechanism evidence is stronger for a controllable exposure/retention tradeoff than for one scalar “mature starvation causes quality loss” chain. Stale-directed allocation can improve fixed-view retention under measured stage-time targets, with saturation and seed variability that depend on scene and growth state. Growth still has a negative scene-conditional quality effect, and raising the stale quota does not reliably neutralize it. Keep the paper claim narrow; next, analyze per-Gaussian residual/revisit strata and repeat at a later snapshot before considering any controller. No held-out or long-horizon claim follows from this run.

### Later-stage dose response and mature residual/revisit strata

The dose-response was repeated after the map had matured: TUM `tum_fr1_rpy` at frame 90 and Replica `replica_office1` at frame 89 (the sequence ends at frame 119, so frame 89 is the latest snapshot with a complete 30-frame horizon). Each scene used eight paired seeds and the same growth × 25/50/75%-stale-rescue arms. The runner now preserves, before each horizon optimization, mature-Gaussian strata by time since last update (0–2, 3–5, 6–9, 10–19, 20+ frames), with visible population fraction, update count, and visible RGB/depth residual. Protocol amendment and boundary note are in the [preregistration](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/late_snapshot_dose_prereg/preregistration.md). Results: [TUM frame 90](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/late_snapshot_dose_tum_fr1_rpy/dose_response_report.md) and [Replica frame 89](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/late_snapshot_dose_replica_office1_frame89/dose_response_report.md). All 16/16 seed blocks passed.

The mature-stage quality response reverses by scene. With zero births, stale rescue changed fixed-view PSNR by −0.143, −0.155, and −0.164 dB at 25/50/75% on TUM (all seed-bootstrap 95% CIs below zero). On Replica, those changes were +0.123 [+0.018, +0.225], +0.173 [+0.012, +0.330], and +0.246 [+0.112, +0.365] dB. At 75% requested quota, realized stale work was 56.8% on TUM and 73.2% on Replica. Thus “stale rescue” is not uniformly beneficial, even when its action and measured stage-time protocol are matched.

The measured residual strata add a plausible explanation to test, but not a conclusion. Before optimization, very stale visible Gaussians had lower average residual than recently updated visible Gaussians in both scenes. At zero growth, TUM's 20+ frame stratum had mean visible RGB/depth residual 0.0538/0.0732, compared with 0–2 frame residual 0.0726/0.2841; Replica's corresponding values were 0.0064/0.0136 versus 0.0180/0.0249. Rescue 75% reduced the visible fraction in the 20+ bin from 15.7% to 2.9% on TUM and from 21.5% to 9.3% on Replica. Yet only Replica gained quality. The residual averages are conditioned on visibility and the intervention changes the bin composition; they are not per-identity causal changes. The evidence therefore rejects “stale means high residual” and points to scene-specific revisit/representation value as a confounder or moderator worth measuring.

At this late stage, adding 3,584 Gaussians raised fixed-view PSNR change by +6.815 dB [6.144, 7.427] on TUM and +3.043 dB [2.703, 3.383] on Replica in the global arm. These frames (70/80/89 or 69/79/88) precede the intervention frame, so the newly spawned Gaussians can directly support the evaluation views. Do not attribute these gains to better update allocation or reduced starvation. Stale rescue at high growth reduced the apparent gain, especially TUM; direct representation support and refinement opportunity are coupled in this endpoint.

**Updated next decision:** do not tune a universal stale quota from these results. The same quota helps at frame 30 but can hurt TUM at the mature stage, while Replica benefits at both stages. Continue Direction A by analyzing per-identity revisit transitions and residual change after an update, then design a contrast that matches visible candidate pool and update count while controlling residual/novelty. Any policy should wait until an untouched sequence validates a scene-state moderator; the current runs remain post hoc, exposed-scene mechanism evidence. Full-frame latency was approximately 99–100 ms on TUM and 76–80 ms on Replica; the stage targets of 85/60 ms do not represent a whole-pipeline budget.

### Frame-level repeatability audit

The same-seed concern was tested against the original uninstrumented factorial runner, using the same config SHA, dirty source revision, TUM sequence request, seed 42, frame-90 snapshot, RTX 4050, and two small action branches. Two valid runs yielded different snapshot hashes (`ac75f559…` and `fa18ca54…`), 68,681 versus 69,163 Gaussians after the zero-birth action, and a 0.076 dB difference in pre-action PSNR. Therefore the mismatch is not only an artifact of enabling trace instrumentation.

An instrumented pair then localized the first mismatch. Initialization forward render, loss, and render-output gradient hashes matched; `init_step_0_gradients` for xyz, scale, opacity, and appearance differed bitwise, with relative L2 differences about 4.7–6.1×10⁻⁸. The post-init map hash differed at input frame 1. RNG digests and update counts stayed equal; Gaussian count and birth count first diverged at frame 17 (one birth). This points to tiny floating-point accumulation differences in the GPU backward path, plausibly involving gsplat backward, which are amplified by later thresholded spawn decisions. The trace does not prove which CUDA operation is responsible. See the [full audit report](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/prefix_repeatability_seed42/report.md).

**Deterministic-mode follow-up:** enabling `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` did not raise an error on the production CUDA/gsplat path, but did not restore repeatability. On two seed-42 runs through input frame 1, initial parameter-gradient tensors still differed at tiny floating-point scale (relative L2 around 5.1–6.2×10⁻⁸), and final map hashes differed despite identical counts (13,151), births (487), update counts (65,755), and post-frame RNG digests. This is consistent with the existing synthetic backend control, where gsplat forward outputs and output adjoints repeat bitwise but per-Gaussian backward gradients do not, while the reference renderer repeated bitwise in that small synthetic test. The reference control is not a full-pipeline proof and says nothing about its practicality at mapping scale. Detailed deterministic-run comparison: [`deterministic_frame1_analysis`](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/prefix_repeatability_seed42/deterministic_frame1_analysis/comparison.md).

**Protocol implication:** within-run branch contrasts remain paired to an exact shared snapshot; rerunning a seed does not regenerate that same snapshot. Seed-bootstrap intervals from one run per seed omit run-to-run trajectory variability. Freeze and archive actual common snapshots for confirmatory branch comparisons, and repeat full prefixes to estimate run-to-run variance. Do not claim that strict PyTorch deterministic mode fixes this path; do not replace gsplat based on this evidence alone.

**Saved-snapshot integrity audit:** the existing same-snapshot factorial runner already deep-copies one live pipeline for each branch, so branches within a seed block begin from the same in-memory state. I added `experiments/audit_map_snapshots.py` to verify optional saved `.pt` map artifacts against their record SHA256 and recomputed Gaussian-map digest. The audit verified all 42 snapshot references in the mechanism-growth/starvation result tree; report: [`snapshot integrity audit`](../results/phase14_generalization_postfix/mechanism_growth_starvation/snapshot_integrity_audit.md). These files are valid map/provenance artifacts, but the format stores Gaussian parameters/state and a small prefix summary only. It omits optimizer momentum and other pipeline state, so it must not be presented as a restartable full-pipeline checkpoint. Existing same-run branch pairing remains sound; reproducing those branches in a later process from the saved `.pt` alone is not yet supported.

To prevent provenance corruption on reruns, both same-snapshot factorial runners now validate an already-existing snapshot against the newly generated prefix before referencing it. A stale/mismatched file raises an error; `--force` explicitly replaces it along with the run artifacts. A stored frame-30 snapshot passed the new read-only validator. Because the original map format omits optimizer, tracker, cache, and RNG state, the separate full replay checkpoint path is described next.

The separate full replay checkpoint path is now implemented in `src/core/pipeline_checkpoint.py`. Both replay runners expose opt-in `--save_replay_checkpoints` and `--replay_checkpoint_dir`; the loader checks scene, seed, frame, effective config hash, Gaussian count, and map digest before restoring Gaussian parameters/state, optimizer moments, tracker, estimator, scheduler, background cache, pipeline counters, and RNG. `experiments/README.md` documents the save/replay commands. ### Cross-scene spawn-128 matched-time replication

Ran the frozen 5-repeat same-snapshot spawn-128 comparison at frame 90 on four Replica office1 seeds (260–263) and four TUM fr1/rpy seeds (264–267). Each arm used 128 births; quota 25% used 5,000 updates and quota 75% used 4,374. All eight snapshots passed map identity, spawn, and exact quota/update gates. A snapshot counted as time matched only if each arm's CUDA-event and synchronized wall CV was ≤5% and the between-quota mean gap was ≤5% for both timers.

Timing gates passed for 3/4 TUM snapshots and 0/4 Replica snapshots. TUM's passing subset (seeds 265–267) had mean PSNR difference +0.26641 dB (seed SD 0.01493) and mean Depth-L1 difference −0.000271 m; PSNR was higher in all three, while depth L1 improved in two. These are four seed snapshots in a development scene, with only three passing the timing gate, so treat as preliminary and do not claim generalization. Replica's descriptive all-seed means were +0.04309 dB PSNR and −0.000774 m depth L1, but no Replica snapshot met the complete timing gate; there is no compute-matched Replica estimate.

Protocol correction: the first combined runner call applied seeds 260–263 to both scenes, contrary to the frozen TUM seed list 264–267. TUM 260–263 outputs are marked exploratory and excluded. TUM 264–267 were rerun separately with a separate manifest. The corrected TUM data and all per-seed gates are covered in the report.

### Replica-specific matched-time repeatability v2

### Real-gsplat gradient fidelity for separable SSIM

### Active-subset first-backward gradient comparison for separable SSIM

Extended `experiments/run_joint_spawn_refine_replay.py` to pair direct and separable branches in one invocation and save the first selected-parameter gradients immediately before `SelectiveAdam.step`. This captures the production path with scheduler-selected Gaussian subsets, frozen-background cache, RGB-D loss, and gsplat backward. It stores the active Gaussian indices and tensor payloads with hashes; paired index hashes and parameter shapes are enforced as validity gates.

Ran six checkpoints across Replica room1 (seeds 279–281, frame 30) and office1 (seeds 264–266, frame 90), crossing spawn 0/128 and three repeats at 50% mature quota. All 36 direct/separable action pairs passed exact index, snapshot, spawn, and update gates. Mean global relative-L2 gradient differences were 7.76e−4 on room1 and 1.77e−3 on office1, with cosine effectively 1.0. Same-implementation repeat variability was about 5.5e−8, so the cross-implementation perturbation is stable and implementation-associated under this protocol. Appearance DC gradients had the largest relative change (mean 1.29e−2); absolute differences remained small.

Interpretation: separable SSIM preserves gradient direction closely but is not numerically identical in the actual active-subset path. This provides a plausible mechanism for eventual trajectory divergence, but one first backward per action does not establish long-horizon quality impact. Keep `direct` as default pending a controlled multi-step trajectory comparison.

### Locked-decision 30-frame SSIM rollout on independent checkpoints

To isolate the numerical gradient perturbation from later map-growth and scheduler feedback, added a fixed-decision mode to `experiments/run_ssim_checkpoint_rollout.py`. For each checkpoint, a direct canonical branch generated per-frame `persistent_id -> k` optimizer allocations. Direct and separable branches replayed that exact schedule from the original checkpoint with spawn/pruning disabled, profiling feedback frozen, and common per-frame RNG. Strict runtime gates required every scheduled ID to resolve, the same persistent-ID roster and map size, zero births, and exactly 5,000 updates per frame.

Ran on two independent room1 checkpoints (seeds 279 and 281, frame 30), three branches per implementation, frames 31–60. All gates passed. Separable-minus-direct final mean deltas were +7.99e−5 / +6.32e−5 dB PSNR, −8.75e−5 / −8.82e−5 SSIM, and −2.46e−6 / −2.50e−6 m Depth L1. Same-method repeated PSNR variability remained below the cross-implementation shift. This is a consistent, extremely small numerical effect with decisions held fixed; it is not evidence of a practically meaningful quality advantage and is limited to two snapshots in one scene.

### Replica E2 spawn-timing transfer pilot

The existing clean E2 rerun covered TUM only. Extended matched-total early-versus-late spawning to Replica room2 and office3, with 119 corrected-pose frames. The initial natural-dose pilot used two seeds per scene and matched exact totals within each pair (about 145k–154k births). A clean fixed-50k cohort then grew to four seeds per scene under both uniform-depth and error-first proposal modes.

### E2 candidate-policy check at fixed dose

Repeated the 50k early/late contrast with an error-mask-first proposal rule and depth-valid fallback, then reran from a clean source snapshot. The broad trajectory-vs-endpoint crossover remained, but effect sizes and Depth L1 changed substantially. Under uniform-depth proposals, late-minus-early mean PSNR was −0.24 dB on room2 and −0.02 dB on office3; mean Depth L1 improved by 0.014/0.021 m. Under error-first, mean PSNR deltas were −1.06/−2.09 dB, while mean Depth L1 worsened by 0.077/0.048 m. Final PSNR favored late spawning in all four scene-policy cells.

This is evidence that the E2 contrast interacts with proposal policy and that endpoint PSNR can disagree with trajectory-average PSNR and depth. Four seeds per scene still give a small exploratory cohort; bootstrap intervals are descriptive. Error-first fills candidate shortfall from valid-depth pixels. Do not reduce this result to “late spawning is better.”
